Who Will Demand a Fast and Just Energy Transition?

Indonesians are not yet ready to demand an energy transition that is both fast and just. The Green Barometer, a survey of 2,033 Indonesian adults, finds three barriers: low energy literacy, a misdirected perceived violation of moral beliefs, and unchanneled politicized identity as defenders of the environment.

Executive summary

Indonesians are not yet ready to demand an energy transition that is both fast and just. The Green Barometer, a survey of 2,033 Indonesian adults, finds three barriers: low energy literacy, a misdirected perceived violation of moral beliefs, and unchanneled politicized identity as defenders of the environment.

Why public demand matters

Indonesia has missed its own renewable energy target every year since at least 2015. In 2025, renewable energy supplied 15.75% of the primary energy mix, against the 23% set in regulation for that year. The government also repeatedly lowered and deferred the target, ultimately ending with Government Regulation (PP) 40/2025, which replaced it with a 19–23% range delayed to 2030. On December 5, 2025, the government canceled the early retirement of the Cirebon-1 coal power plant. These actions are inconsistent with the president’s November 2024 pledge to phase out coal-fired and other fossil-fueled power plants within 15 years and to reach net-zero emissions by 2050.

Yet, speed alone is not enough. The transition will displace communities and put tens of thousands of jobs at risk. It must therefore also be just: benefits and burdens shared fairly, affected communities involved in decisions, and vulnerable groups supported. A transition that pushes its costs onto powerless groups may lose public support, private funding, and the ability to survive legal challenges.

Both the speed and the fairness of the transition are political choices. If nobody watches them closely, nothing changes, and the status quo continues. When the government cut the 2025 target, energy-sector civil society organizations (CSOs) protested, but they had no broader public behind them. Sustained public demand cannot guarantee change. But without it, the government pays no political price when it reverses a policy or fails to carry it out.

How we measured intent for public demand

The Green Barometer is a biennial tracking study of opinions and attitudes on climate change, the energy transition, and forest and land use. This first wave surveyed 2,033 Indonesian adults, drawn by stratified random sampling across 19 urban and 18 rural locations. Respondents first answered questions on climate change and energy transition vocabulary. They then read plain-language definitions before giving their opinions.

In our analysis, we conceptualize the intent for public demand as the intention to participate in collective action—defined as citizens organizing collectively to advocate for policy changes via channels like social media, petitions, or rallies. We used the Social Identity Model of Collective Action (SIMCA), which names four psychological predictors: politicized identity (a sense of belonging to a group that engages in a power struggle to make change), perceived violation of moral beliefs, group-based anger, and collective efficacy (the belief that the group can make change happen). We added two factors that may matter for the energy transition: understanding of basic concepts related to the transition, and perceived religious obligation (the subjective belief that one’s religion prescribes protecting the environment).

Reported past participation in collective action is low. Across nine forms of environmental collective action, most respondents had never taken part. The most common form, donating to an environmental organization, was reported by only 17%. Participation has not changed significantly since a 2021 survey, except for one category: joining a boycott (from 5% to 12%). Stated intention to join was higher than past behavior, but self-reported intention often overstates what people later do.

Four factors predicted intention to join: energy literacy, perceived violation of moral beliefs, perceived religious obligation, and politicized identity. Politicized identity was the strongest predictor. The report does not analyze religious obligation further and leaves it to other scholars. The three barriers below follow from the three predictors this report analyzes: energy literacy, perceived violation of moral beliefs, and politicized identity.

Three barriers hold the public back

Each barrier ties to one predictor of collective action. Together they explain why public demand for a fast and just energy transition is still absent.

Low energy literacy
  • The average energy literacy score is 3.8 on a scale from -33 to 35, and the median is 2.
  • Only 28% have heard the term “renewable energy” and 24% have heard the term “energy transition”.
  • 41% do not choose the sun as a renewable energy source.
  • Only 17% name burning coal as a cause of climate change, even after a plain-language explanation.
  • Among respondents with high energy literacy, 78% want the government to speed up the transition immediately, compared with 58% among those with low literacy.
People cannot demand what they do not understand, and they are less able to judge the government’s effort as a failure. Energy literacy raises support for green policies, but it does not change the roughly 50% who say fuel subsidies must stay a top priority.
A misdirected perceived violation of moral beliefs
  • 87% name the national government as most responsible for the transition, and 65% say its efforts are insufficient, against 27% who say they are enough.
  • Yet fewer than 20% feel wronged or sacrificed, and half feel calm, happy, or grateful.

Few citizens see the government’s weak effort as a violation of their moral beliefs, so little anger builds. Instead, they aim their sense of unfairness at workers who may lose coal or oil jobs, because they may think these workers can help themselves.
Unchanneled politicized identity
  • 55% to 57% would feel proud to be seen as part of a group supporting or demanding an energy transition, yet only 10% of the general public have taken part.

Citizens who are willing to act may not see a channel they trust. Because they do not take part, they never gain the experience that builds trust and the belief that action works. This creates a vicious cycle.

Section 1

Demanding an energy transition that is both fast and just

Indonesia has missed its own renewable energy target every year since at least 2015, reaching 15.75% of the primary energy mix in 2025 against the 23% set in regulation for that year. Consequently, the country is on track to fall short of its president’s pledge, made before G20 leaders in November 2024, to phase out coal-fired and fossil-fueled power plants within 15 years and achieve net-zero emissions by 2050. There was another setback: the government officially canceled the early retirement of the PLTU Cirebon-1 coal power plant on December 5, 2025. If Indonesia is genuinely committed, it must significantly quicken its energy transition.

However, speed alone is not enough, as the transition involves massive shifts that will affect lives and livelihoods. Building renewable energy facilities can displace communities, while tens of thousands of workers risk losing their jobs. Indeed, evidence from emerging economies like India and Mexico shows that rapid renewable expansion often enriches landowners while stripping landless populations of common resources and wage labor.

Therefore, the transition must also be just: its benefits and burdens must be shared fairly, affected communities must participate in decision-making, and vulnerable populations must be supported.1 A transition that puts most of its costs on powerless groups may lose public support, private funding, and the ability to survive legal challenges. In some documented cases, perceived injustice has already led to suspended project funding.

With nobody scrutinizing, the pace of transition and the fairness of its outcomes are political choices that default to maintaining the status quo. For instance, the government repeatedly lowered and deferred its 2025 renewable target, most recently through Government Regulation (PP) 40/2025, which replaced the target with a range of 19–23%, to be reached by 2030. Although energy-sector CSOs protested the initial cuts, their efforts lacked broader public backing.

At times, citizen mobilization in Indonesia has forced the state to change or cancel policies, especially on governance and election integrity. However, it is much less effective against powerful, long-established extractive industries. Sustained public demand cannot guarantee that political inertia will be overcome. But without it, policy reversals and non-implementation face no political cost. Building public demand addresses this gap.

This raises a crucial question: Is the Indonesian public currently ready to demand an energy transition that is both fast and just?

The short answer is no, at least not yet. The remainder of this report examines the underlying causes of this unreadiness. Section 2 presents the survey, shows how low participation in collective action is, and introduces the model we use to explain it. Sections 3 to 5 take up the three barriers in turn. Section 3 shows that the public has a poor understanding of the basic concepts of the energy transition. Section 4 shows that few citizens see the government’s insufficient efforts as a violation of their moral beliefs, yet many disapprove of social assistance for a group that could become the “sacrificial lamb” of the transition. Section 5 asks whether trust in CSOs is the missing link between citizens’ strong sense of belonging to a group that demands social change and their low levels of participation in collective action. Finally, Section 6 sets out what CSOs and funders should do about each barrier. The notes on methodology describe the survey, the analysis, and the questionnaires.

Section 2

Measuring intention to demand a fast and just energy transition

To evaluate whether the Indonesian public is ready to demand a fast and just energy transition, we conducted the Green Barometer, a biennial tracking study of opinions and attitudes toward climate change, the energy transition, and forest and land use. In this inaugural wave, we used stratified random sampling to survey 2,033 Indonesian adults across 19 urban and 18 rural locations. We first measured respondents’ basic knowledge of the energy transition, including whether they knew and understood common terms. We then showed them plain-language definitions of key concepts before asking for their opinions.

Citizen demand is still low

We define intention to demand as the willingness to take part in collective action. Collective action happens when people work together to demand policy changes from the government through social media, petitions, or protests. Instead of acting alone, individuals unite around common goals to drive social change.

Our survey asked whether respondents had ever participated in nine forms of collective action related to protecting the environment from man-made degradation. The answer was not encouraging. We found that for each activity, 81% to 95% of respondents had never participated. Donating money to an environmental organization was the most common activity, but only 17% of respondents had ever done so. See Figure 1 below:

We compared the data with a 2021 survey collecting similar information, and found no significant changes.2 Participation in the same actions was similarly low in 2021. There was one exception, with a significant increase. The rate of people joining a boycott rose from 5% in 2021 to 12% in 2026. We suspect this increase reflects the popularity of the Boycott, Divestment, Sanctions (BDS) campaign against companies linked to Israel. This campaign spread in Indonesia from around October 2023, even though it was not an environmental boycott.

We also asked the respondents about their future intention to join the same collective action. Intention was higher than reported past behavior, as Figure 3 shows. Self-reported intentions do not always become actual behavior, but the results show how low past participation has been.

Because very few citizens are currently taking action, we need to understand what drives them to do so.

SIMCA to explain and predict collective action

To understand what drives or hinders collective action, we draw on the Social Identity Model of Collective Action (SIMCA). The model identifies four psychological “engines” that can lead individuals to participate in collective action:

  • The first is politicized identity: seeing oneself as part of a group that deliberately takes part in a struggle for power on behalf of its members. It is a shared identity as “activists,” even if members do not say so openly.
  • The second is perceived violation of moral beliefs. Moral beliefs are a person’s views about what is right and wrong and about what she and her group stand for. Perceived violation, in turn, concerns whether those beliefs are breached or threatened. It is the “active ingredient” that moves people to protect the belief through collective action.
  • The third is group-based anger that people feel when their group believes it is treated unjustly.
  • The fourth is the conviction that the group can actually bring about change, or collective efficacy.

We added two factors that we hypothesized can also influence collective action related to climate mitigation or energy transition:

  • Level of energy literacy: a score indicating an individual’s understanding of the basic concepts in energy transition, such as fossil fuels, renewable energy, and energy transition itself. We calculated the score by checking whether a respondent’s answers to several survey questions match the experts’ consensus.
  • Perceived religious obligation: a measure of how strongly a person believes that her religion, as she understands it, makes it her personal duty to act. In our case, this means protecting the environment from damage caused by humans. Some scholars argue that SIMCA should include a broader set of moral beliefs and be tested in different cultures. Following them, we measured perceived religious obligation as a moral belief that is important in Indonesia, where morality is widely grounded in religion.

Of the six factors (4 from SIMCA and 2 that we added), four predicted intention to join environmental-protection collective action (see Figure 5). Group-based anger and collective efficacy did not predict intention; efficacy was linked only to past participation. The four predictors are:

  • Energy literacy predicted intention. Unfortunately, citizens’ average level of energy literacy is low. People cannot demand a fast and just energy transition if they do not know what it means, what it costs, whom it benefits, or what other choices exist.
  • Perceived violation of moral beliefs predicted intention. Even so, only a minority of respondents viewed the government’s insufficient effort as a violation of their moral beliefs. Most Indonesians held the government responsible for the transition. Most believed the government’s efforts in this agenda were paltry. Yet most did not feel wronged by it. Instead, their sense of unfairness was aimed at the people who may be “sacrificed” in the transition. They believed that displaced workers did not deserve social assistance.
  • Perceived religious obligation predicted intention. Because we have limited expertise on religion, we do not discuss this variable in this report. We hope other scholars will further explore the relationship between religiosity and collective action on non-religious issues.
  • Politicized identity predicted intention. Our model showed it was the strongest predictor of intention. On average, citizens had a strong sense of belonging to such a group. Yet, surprisingly, they did not take part in collective action. We propose that low trust in CSOs, measured in our separate 2025 democracy and civic space study, may explain why highly politicized identity has not led to action.

Beyond these four predictors, a self-reported history of participating in environmental collective action also predicted future participation intent. People who have taken part in more types of action also tended to have a stronger politicized identity. They were more likely to see government action on climate change and the energy transition as a violation of their moral beliefs, and they felt a stronger perceived religious obligation to act. These matched the predictors of intention to take part in collective action.

Past participants were also more inclined to believe in the efficacy of collective action. Although it is unclear whether this belief comes before or after participation, we hypothesize the latter: taking part in action builds a sense of collective efficacy. This perspective is consistent with behavioral science research indicating that past behavior influences current attitudes, rather than vice versa.

On the other hand, we found no evidence that energy literacy was related to past participation, although we cannot completely rule out a small effect. People with a history of participation did not necessarily have higher energy literacy than non-participants.

The next three sections take up the barriers that SIMCA and our added factors point to, starting with energy literacy.

Section 3

The first barrier: low energy literacy

Most Indonesians lack energy literacy

We measured respondents’ knowledge of the energy transition. We calculated these scores by combining several survey questions with right-or-wrong answers. We gave respondents 1 point (+1) for an answer that reflects the experts’ consensus, 0 points (0) for a “don’t know” response, and -1 point (-1) for an answer that goes against the experts’ consensus. For multiple-answer questions, respondents could earn or lose multiple points.

The Indonesian public has very low energy literacy. Across ten survey items, scores ranged from -16 to 29 (within the possible range of -33 to 35), with an average score of 3.8 and a median score of 2. This means that half of respondents scored 2 or below. We found that most energy literacy scores clustered heavily at the lower end of the scale (Figure 6). We detail our full scoring construction in the Notes on methodology.

Low energy literacy shows up in several ways. Firstly, most citizens lack basic awareness. Only 28% of Indonesians have ever heard the term “renewable energy”, and only 24% have ever heard the term “energy transition” (see Figure 7).

Secondly, many do not see fossil fuels as a problem: 35% viewed them positively and another 30% neutrally. To be fair, renewable energy and the energy transition were viewed more favorably: more respondents saw them positively and fewer saw them negatively (Figure 8).

Thirdly, respondents often name the wrong sources of fossil fuels or renewable energy, even when they could read each definition again and again during the interview. For example, 41% of respondents did not choose the sun as a renewable energy source. See Figure 9:

Fourthly, we found that the public wrongly blames renewable energy for environmental problems. Specifically, around 20% of respondents attributed climate change, pollution, energy scarcity, and natural disasters to renewable energy rather than to fossil fuels. Another 24% of respondents believed that renewable energy harmed the environment just as much as fossil fuels did. See Figure 10:

Fifthly, our findings showed that almost no citizens linked burning coal and other fossil fuels directly to climate change. Before being shown a plain-language explanation, only 7% of participants cited emissions from burning petroleum or coal as a cause of climate change. Even after the explanation, the share identifying coal rose to just 17%, while petrol reached 12%. These figures are dwarfed by alternative explanations: 72% pointed to natural cycles, 70% attributed it to shifting weather patterns, 58% blamed deforestation, and 56% assigned responsibility to divine intervention. See Figure 11:

Sixthly, most people do not see energy transition steps as effective actions against climate change. To the public, the most impactful climate actions are forest restoration (83%), mangrove planting (46%), and peatland restoration (38%). Reducing oil and coal use ranked fifth (30%), while building solar power plants ranked eighth (13%)—behind artificial rain (18%) and sea walls (17%). See Figure 12:

Awareness of renewable energy is rising slowly among urban youth

But there is encouraging news. For the one measure we can compare over time, Indonesia has made progress. In a 2021 online survey, we asked urban Indonesians aged 16 to 39 if they had heard the term “renewable energy”. At that time, 23% of respondents had heard of it. In our 2026 survey, we matched this group by looking only at urban respondents under 40. We found that this share rose to 36%, which represents an increase of 13 percentage points in five years.3 However, across all respondents, including rural and older ones, awareness is 28%. See Figure 13:

Energy literacy increases support for green policies, but does not change views on fuel subsidies

Another encouraging finding is that people with higher energy literacy are more likely to support energy transition policies. When asked whether the government should speed up the transition immediately, 78% of high-literacy respondents said yes, compared with 58% of low-literacy respondents. This represents a substantial 20-percentage-point gap (Figure 14):

In addition, 58% of high-literacy respondents supported building renewable energy plants, compared with 47% of low-literacy respondents. Unfortunately, energy literacy did not change a citizen’s views on fuel subsidies. About 50% of respondents in both the high- and low-literacy groups believed that keeping fuel (BBM) subsidies to hold prices down must remain a top government priority (Figure 15). We suspect that this occurs because fuel subsidies directly affect monthly household budgets.

We expected literacy to sharpen citizens’ moral reaction to the government’s shortcomings, since understanding energy transition concepts makes it easier to see why slow progress matters. However, the data showed only a modest effect. We asked respondents who perceive a responsibility-action gap (the party seen as most responsible is not the one seen as most active) how disturbed they feel about it. Among high-literacy respondents, 75% felt at least somewhat disturbed, compared with 68% of low-literacy respondents (Figure 16).

In other words, higher energy literacy is associated with only a modest increase in the likelihood of seeing the government’s deficient action as a violation of moral beliefs, mostly because low-literacy respondents are more likely to feel no disturbance at all. It is also important to note that fewer than half of all respondents (47%) recognized this responsibility-action gap in the first place. This leads us to the second barrier: a misdirected perceived violation of moral beliefs.

Section 4

The second barrier: perceived violation of moral beliefs directed to the wrong actors

Most Indonesians see the government’s efforts as insufficient, but do not feel wronged by them

The Indonesian public strongly agreed on who should lead the energy transition. When we asked respondents who is most responsible for the energy transition, 87% named the national government and 57% the local government. These shares far exceed those of other actors, including the private sector, society as a whole, individuals, international organizations, researchers, and CSOs. In addition, the public also saw the government—again, both national and local—as the actor most active in pushing for the energy transition. See Figure 17:

About a third of respondents said the national and local governments are most responsible for the transition, but not the most active in making it happen (33% and 31% respectively). See Figure 18:

We asked respondents who see this responsibility-action gap how they feel about it. About 3 in 4 people who saw the gap said it bothered them, but the intensity was moderate. “Somewhat disturbed” was the most common answer, and only ~10% were “very disturbed”. See Figure 19:

While most believed the government was the most responsible and active party in pushing the energy transition, more than half (65%) saw its efforts as insufficient. This is much bigger than the 27% who thought that the government had done enough. Predictably, those who perceived a responsibility-action gap were more likely to deem these efforts insufficient. See Figure 20:

Although respondents perceived the government’s efforts as deficient, most did not experience negative moral emotions. To capture feelings toward the government’s overall effort, we asked all respondents to choose a point on a 7-point scale with opposing moral emotions at each end, such as feeling victimized vs. protected, and being treated fairly vs. unfairly. Fewer than 20% felt wronged or sacrificed. In contrast, half of respondents felt calm, happy, or even grateful about government efforts. See Figure 21:

Why do most Indonesians view government efforts as inadequate without perceiving this shortfall as a violation of their moral beliefs? We propose two explanations for this disconnect:

  • Low energy literacy: Limited understanding of what the transition requires may blunt the sense that slow progress matters, although the gap between high- and low-literacy respondents is modest (Figure 16). This is reflected in the finding that a mere 7% of participants identified emissions from coal or petroleum combustion as a driver of climate change before being shown a plain-language explanation (Figure 11 shows responses after it).
  • Acceptance of the “development requires sacrifice” narrative: Citizens who accept the idea that development requires hardship may be more tolerant of injustice or harm caused by what the state does or fails to do. A study of eight decades of presidential speeches shows that the government has consistently presented progress as something that requires sacrifice. Similarly, a 2021 study showed that 60% of Indonesians were willing to accept widespread deforestation if it brought economic gains.

In the SIMCA framework, a weak perceived violation of moral beliefs (caused by low energy literacy, acceptance of the “development requires sacrifice” narrative, or both) is a weak basis for mobilizing the public. This helps explain the low level of participation in pro-environmental collective action across Indonesia.

Yet the public is not indifferent to fairness. Our study shows that people do sense unfairness in one part of a just energy transition, but they direct it at the wrong target.

The public may see people who would lose jobs due to the transition as undeserving of social assistance

The energy transition will end coal jobs4. One estimate suggests that the energy transition puts 30,000 Indonesian mining workers at risk of layoff between 2020 and 2040. In mining areas with no other major industry, these losses will devastate the local economy. A just energy transition therefore requires us to help those whose livelihoods are at risk.

To examine how the public would react to assisting those who will lose jobs due to the transition, we gave respondents several household scenarios that differ in families’ living conditions, incomes, possessions, and lifestyles. For each household, we asked respondents how strongly they agreed that it should receive social assistance.

The survey showed strong agreement across Indonesia on who should get social help. Almost everyone agreed that low-income families qualified for aid, regardless of what they own. A large majority—four out of five people (81%)—supported giving aid to vulnerable or near-poor families. Owning basic goods did not reduce sympathy for poor families: 94% said a poor family still deserved aid even if they owned a TV, fridge, or motorcycle. In contrast, more than 90% of respondents said rich or established middle-class families should not get support (Figure 22):

However, this agreement breaks down for a middle-class household whose breadwinner has just lost their job. Our vignette described Mr. Andi, a 38-year-old man who had just been dismissed (dipecat) from the factory where he had worked for eight years. We did not say what the factory produced or why he lost his job. His wife works part-time as a minimarket cashier. Before the dismissal, the family lived comfortably on a joint income of about Rp 10 million a month. Now they are postponing payments on a new motorcycle, eating out less, and drawing on their savings. Only 44% said this household deserved help, while 56% said it did not.

Coal workers who lose their jobs may look much like Mr. Andi’s family to the public. Mining and quarrying is one of Indonesia’s best-paid sectors. In the Kalimantan mining communities that Großmann and Gullo (2022) studied, people see a mine job as the route to a better life. If the public sees laid-off coal workers as at least as comfortable as Mr. Andi’s family, many would likely judge them just as harshly. This is our inference, not a finding: respondents judged a worker from an unnamed factory, not a coal worker.

We suspect that the public applies a “perfect victim” test, close to what the criminologist Nils Christie called the “ideal victim” (Remotivi discusses the idea in the Indonesian context). Under this test, a person deserves help only when two conditions are true: the hardship is completely outside their control, and they have no other way to help themselves. Our survey shows that support drops for Mr. Andi’s household, but it cannot show why. By our reading, the household fails the second condition: the family lived comfortably before, still has savings, and still has his wife’s income. What they are cutting back on are comforts, not necessities. To the public, they are not yet poor, so they can still help themselves. Laid-off coal workers would likely fail the second condition in the same way.

Seen this way, the public may be reluctant to support a just energy transition if it means asking for compensation for people they believe do not deserve help. Ordinary people may define social justice differently than we do, so CSOs cannot assume that fairness arguments will persuade them. We recommend that CSOs redirect the public’s sense of unfairness toward the government’s failure to manage the transition properly, while convincing the public to support the workers who bear the highest costs.

But even citizens who sense unfairness and are willing to act on it need a trusted channel to act through. That is the third barrier.

Section 5

The third barrier: low trust in CSOs may hinder citizens with strong politicized identity

Most Indonesians have high politicized identity, yet collective action participation remains low

As mentioned in Section 2, the strongest predictor of a person’s willingness to take collective action is politicized identity: seeing oneself as part of a group that supports environmental protection and demands government action. We found that politicized identity ran high among the Indonesian public. Specifically, 55% to 57% of respondents said they would feel proud if others saw them as part of a group supporting or demanding an energy transition, while only a minority would object (Figure 23). These results seem to suggest that a majority of Indonesians already view themselves as having a highly politicized identity.

Although many people score high on politicized identity, actual participation in collective environmental action remains low, with 81% to 95% of respondents never having participated in any of the nine forms of collective action.

We tried to explain this puzzle by comparing respondents with high and low politicized identity across every topic we studied: climate change, energy transition, and forest recovery. We expected respondents with high politicized identity to show the highest participation. Yet, on average, their past participation was only about 12% across the nine actions (about 1 action per person), compared with 8% (about 0.7 actions each) in the low politicized identity group. Additionally, even among those with a high level of politicized identity, they intended to take part in only 44% of the actions (about 4 actions each) in the future. This is higher than the 31% (about 2.8 actions each) among the low politicized identity group, but not that much. It’s also worth repeating that intentions don’t always translate to real action. See Figure 24:

This analysis suggests that the main barrier to participation is not only whether citizens have a highly politicized identity, but also what happens after they become willing to act.

Low trust in available organizations leaves citizens without a channel to act

CSOs are the main platforms for building a politicized identity and a belief in collective action. In social movement theory, groups that stand between individuals and the state can turn personal grievances into organized public demand. They build collective efficacy by achieving real victories. In this process, CSO leaders act as identity entrepreneurs and dealers of hope.

However, people are far more likely to join collective action when they trust the organization leading it. This presents a major challenge for civil society groups in Indonesia. In our 2025 democracy and civic space study, we asked respondents how much they trust five types of organizations to help them submit policy suggestions to the government, express complaints, or protest decisions. These questions are about civic space in general, not energy, and come from a separate survey, so we treat them as an indirect indicator. See Figure 25:

We found that no organization type earned the full trust of even 33% of the public, and CSOs do not lead. Religion-based organizations ranked first across all three actions, earning trust from 32% of respondents for policy suggestions, 32% for complaints, and 29% for protests. CSOs ranked second, earning trust from 26% of respondents for suggestions, 25% for complaints, and 22% for protests. Furthermore, trust fell across all organization types as civic action became more confrontational. Every group scored lowest on protest and highest on policy suggestions.

Low trust may also keep itself going. We hypothesize that collective efficacy results from participating in collective action, not the other way around, although a single survey wave cannot establish the order. Participation may also build trust in the groups that organize collective action, such as CSOs, though we could not test this because the trust data come from a separate survey. In contrast, people who have never taken part have no chance to build that trust and may hesitate to join. They may never hear success stories, which keeps their trust low. This can become a vicious cycle.

But there are ways to prevent this from becoming a vicious cycle. People can still hear about social change from others, and these stories can strengthen their belief that they can make change happen. CSOs can share tangible local wins and success stories to demonstrate to non-participants that collective action can succeed and that an organization can deliver. By making their wins visible in local communities, CSOs can become the trusted channels that citizens currently lack. The final section sets out what CSOs and funders can do about each of the three barriers.

Section 6

CSOs and funders must address literacy, fairness, and trust

Each of the three barriers calls for a different response. Below, we set out what CSOs and what funders should do about each one. Because the findings come from a single survey wave, treat the recommendations as informed hypotheses to test, not proven remedies.

Strategic recommendations for CSOs

Overcoming Barrier 1: CSOs should start with building basic energy literacy first

We recommend three actions for CSOs as they build the public’s basic energy literacy:

  1. Build basic energy literacy: Citizens need this in order to judge the government’s effort and fairness in pursuing a fast and just energy transition. Without energy literacy, citizens may not see how far the government’s performance falls short of what the transition requires. This may help explain why their disapproval stays mild, although the difference between high- and low-literacy respondents in Section 3 is small.
  2. Use concrete terms like coal and oil instead of “energy transition”: Only a quarter of Indonesians have ever heard the term “energy transition”. Even when provided with a written definition, many respondents failed to identify renewable and fossil fuel sources. We advise campaigns to focus on visible, concrete sources of damage, specifically burning coal and oil, to establish basic understanding.
  3. Show empathy for household fuel budgets: Citizens with higher energy literacy support more green policies, but it does not change their need for fuel subsidies. Knowledge does not remove people’s worries about money, so campaigns must respect household financial limits instead of arguing with facts alone.

Overcoming Barrier 2: CSOs must reframe environmental costs as failures and redirect support for displaced workers

We recommend two reframing strategies to shift public judgments on fairness:

  1. Frame environmental costs as government failure: The state has framed environmental damage as a necessary sacrifice for national development. We suspect this leads citizens to view environmental damage as an inevitable price of progress rather than as government failure. We recommend that every campaign message about energy transition explicitly identifies who made the decision, who will enjoy the most benefit, and who will bear the highest cost.
  2. Frame social assistance for displaced workers as earned compensation, not charity: Many Indonesians believe a laid-off middle-class household does not deserve social assistance. We infer that displaced coal workers would fail the public’s “perfect victim” test too. We recommend that CSOs frame social assistance for affected workers as a “livelihood bridge” earned through workers’ prior contributions to the nation’s energy.

Overcoming Barrier 3: CSOs should develop public trust through community organizing

We recommend that CSOs invest in community organizing, not persuasion alone. The public already shows highly politicized identities. But highly politicized identity alone does not drive collective action, and we propose that low trust in the available organizations is the reason. We argue that CSOs must focus on community organizing, which requires a sustained local presence, credible local messengers, clear accountability, and visible local wins.

Strategic recommendations for funders

Overcoming Barrier 1: Funders should invest in public literacy alongside policy advocacy

We recommend two funding strategies to build lasting public demand:

  1. Support building public literacy to protect policy wins: Advocacy can win policy commitments from the government, but public vigilance (watching closely) works like insurance, helping those commitments survive. When Indonesia repeatedly lowered its renewable energy target, only a small circle of expert organizations objected, and no wider public joined them. We suspect low literacy is part of the reason. We recommend that funders invest in public energy literacy so citizens can recognize when policy commitments are broken.
  2. Specify the right literacy metrics and set realistic outcomes: We observed that awareness of energy terms is growing among urban youth, but basic understanding remains low. Reach and recall are therefore poor measures of success. We advise funders to require grantees to measure whether citizens can identify renewable energy sources and connect fossil fuel use to climate change. Funders should also set realistic expectations: literacy can increase support for policies without changing people’s attachment to fuel subsidies.

Overcoming Barrier 2: Funders should support counter-narratives and early fairness claims before major displacement occurs

We recommend two funding strategies on social justice:

  1. Treat counter-narratives as a long-term investment: The dominant narrative that development requires sacrifice has persisted for eight decades and is now widely accepted by citizens. A counter-narrative such as “development should ensure that its benefits reach the most disadvantaged without requiring anyone to bear the cost of progress” cannot replace it overnight. We recommend that funders commit to multi-year funding to give counter-narratives time to become established.
  2. Fund fairness messaging before the displacements begin: An estimated 30,000 coal mining workers face layoff risks between 2020 and 2040, while 56% of respondents say a laid-off middle-class household does not deserve social assistance. We cannot achieve a just energy transition if displaced workers are sacrificed in the process. We recommend funding fairness messaging now, before displacement becomes widespread.

Overcoming Barrier 3: Funders should provide infrastructure for community organizing

We recommend that funders provide infrastructure for community organizing efforts. Community organizing builds strong, lasting community groups, but its results are hard to see within short funding periods. We recommend that funders provide multi-year funding to organizations with a strong reputation in their local communities so they can become trusted support structures for collective action.

Taken together, these actions address the three barriers in turn. Literacy helps citizens judge the government’s effort. Fairness framing aims their sense of unfairness at the right target. Community organizing gives them a channel they trust.

Appendix

Notes on methodology

Survey methodology

What the study covers

We interviewed 2,033 Indonesian adults face-to-face between April 6 and May 5, 2026, across 19 urban and 18 rural areas on six major islands: Sumatra, Java, Bali-Nusa Tenggara, Kalimantan, Sulawesi, and Papua. This is the first wave of the Green Barometer, our biennial tracking study of public attitudes and behavior on sustainable and equitable development. The Green Barometer covers sustainable development topics broadly, not energy alone. Respondents received energy questions alongside questions on climate change, forests, social assistance, and other development priorities.

How we selected our respondents

We used stratified random sampling built on kelurahan (urban wards) and desa (rural wards). Within each selected city or regency, we randomly selected kelurahan or desa. Within each ward, we selected two or three Rukun Tetangga (RT; neighborhood units) at random. Enumerators then visited households door-to-door in these RTs to identify eligible respondents: Indonesian citizens aged 18 and over who could hold the interview in Indonesian. Finally, enumerators interviewed the household member whose birthday was closest to the interview date.

How we collected the data

Our fieldwork partner, Cimigo, conducted computer-assisted personal interviews (CAPI) on tablets in respondents’ homes. Each interview lasted about 60–80 minutes. Enumerators used show cards and printed information sheets to display long multiple-answer lists and plain-language definitions. We did not assume respondents knew the vocabulary. For each concept (climate change, fossil fuels, renewable energy, energy transition), we first asked whether respondents had heard the term and what they understood it to mean. We then gave them a short, plain-language definition to read for up to one minute. After providing the definition, we asked follow-up knowledge questions and measured their opinions.

The Center for Ethics Development at Universitas Katolik Indonesia Atma Jaya reviewed our research protocol. Before every interview, enumerators read a consent script covering the study’s purpose, confidentiality, the right to stop at any time, and compensation for the respondent’s time. Respondents then chose whether to participate or not. Cimigo provided us with anonymized data only. In line with our commitment to open science, we will publish the anonymized dataset on LabNarasi.

What predicts the intention to join collective action

We regressed the standardized intention score on the constructs of SIMCA: politicized identity, perceived violation of moral beliefs, group-based anger, and collective efficacy. We added energy literacy and perceived religious obligation, the two factors discussed in Section 2, as well as climate literacy and religious fatalism. We included these last two as additional variables; the main text does not discuss them, so it refers to six factors. The model uses ordinary least squares on the 1,736 respondents with complete data.

We estimated three specifications:

  1. Core: SIMCA constructs, literacy, and religiosity.
  2. With demographics: adds sex, generation, socioeconomic status, education level, media usage, urban or rural residence, residence in an extractive-economy province, and time zone.
  3. Robustness: the same as the “With demographics” specification, but using Java vs. non-Java instead of time zone.

The models explained 19% to 25% of the variation in intention to join collective action.

Enumerator effects and how we corrected for them

Answers were clustered by enumerator (answers from the same enumerator were similar) much more than usual. After controlling for provinces, enumerators accounted for 45% of the variation in energy literacy, 43% in intention, 30% in politicized identity and collective efficacy, and 23% in climate literacy. A face-to-face survey in Uganda that randomly assigned enumerators found that enumerators accounted for only 0.5% to 4% of the variation for most questions and 3% to 6% for demographic questions.

Because clustering reduces the amount of independent information and widens margins of error from ±2 to ±5 percentage points, we tested every association in two ways:

  • Clustered margins of error: We calculated the clustered margin of error (CR2) with Satterthwaite corrections to maintain reliability across small enumerator groups.
  • Within-enumerator comparison: We compared only respondents interviewed by the same enumerator.

We treated an association as robust only if it was still statistically significant at the 5% level under both methods (CR2 and within-enumerator comparison). We report only robust associations.

What the findings do not show

Several of the report’s explanations are inferences, not findings. Please read them as hypotheses to test in later waves.

  1. Intentions are self-reported, and the report notes they do not always become action.
  2. This is a single survey wave. It shows associations, not cause and effect. The report suspects that taking part builds the belief that action works, not the reverse, but it cannot tell which comes first.
  3. The explanation for the gap between high politicized identity and low participation, namely low trust in CSOs, is our hypothesis that has not been tested. The trust data come from a separate 2025 democracy and civic space study of 2,520 respondents.
  4. Respondents judged a dismissed factory worker, not a coal worker. The authors infer that support drops because respondents apply a “perfect victim” test, and that laid-off coal workers would be judged the same way.
  5. The link between the “development requires sacrifice” narrative and public tolerance of unfairness is a hypothesis to be tested. The report also leaves perceived religious obligation unanalyzed.
